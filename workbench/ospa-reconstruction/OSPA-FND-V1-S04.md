## Section 4 — Operational Domains and Hierarchy

### 4.1 Purpose

This section establishes the approved conceptual architecture for operational domains and their hierarchical organization within Huerta Group.

Its purpose is to define how operational subject areas are conceptually organized, bounded, related, specialized, and governed while preserving explicit institutional authority, semantic integrity, and architectural coherence.

Operational domains exist to organize institutional operational understanding.

They do not establish organizational departments, reporting structures, staffing models, runtime systems, or operational execution.

This section authorizes conceptual domain architecture only.

It does not authorize:

* organizational units;
* permanent offices;
* staffing assignments;
* operational execution;
* runtime domain administration;
* production systems;
* implementation structures;
* software modules;
* technical ownership.

---

### 4.2 Definition of an Operational Domain

An operational domain is a governed conceptual boundary established to organize a coherent area of operational meaning under explicit institutional governance.

Each operational domain exists to preserve:

* conceptual coherence;
* semantic stability;
* architectural integrity;
* explicit authority relationships;
* reviewability;
* historical preservation.

A domain may define:

* conceptual purpose;
* conceptual scope;
* governing authority;
* architectural boundaries;
* relationships;
* dependencies;
* interfaces;
* limitations.

A domain does not establish:

* execution authority;
* organizational ownership;
* operational activity;
* technical implementation;
* staffing;
* commercial responsibility.

Operational domains remain conceptual until separately implemented under appropriate institutional authority.

---

### 4.3 Purpose of Domain Architecture

Domain architecture provides a structured conceptual organization through which operational knowledge may be governed without ambiguity.

The architecture supports:

* explicit institutional boundaries;
* specialization;
* disciplined governance;
* controlled relationships;
* modular evolution;
* independent review;
* historical continuity.

Domain architecture exists to improve institutional understanding.

It shall not be interpreted as an organizational chart, software architecture, or operational workflow.

---

### 4.4 Domain Independence

Each operational domain possesses its own conceptual identity.

Domain independence preserves:

* explicit governance;
* conceptual integrity;
* architectural stability;
* reviewability;
* bounded evolution.

Shared terminology, technology, personnel, repositories, or recurring activity shall not merge operational domains.

Domain independence does not prohibit collaboration.

Rather, collaboration occurs through explicitly governed relationships while preserving independent authority and conceptual meaning.

---

### 4.5 Domain Scope

Every operational domain shall possess an explicitly governed scope.

Domain scope identifies the conceptual boundaries within which the domain is authoritative.

Scope may describe:

* governed concepts;
* conceptual responsibilities;
* included subject matter;
* excluded subject matter;
* architectural interfaces;
* dependencies;
* governing limitations.

Scope shall remain conceptually explicit.

Undefined scope shall not be inferred through implementation, organizational practice, repository structure, or historical usage.

---

### 4.6 Domain Boundaries

Operational boundaries preserve the distinction between adjacent conceptual domains.

Boundaries identify where conceptual authority begins and ends.

Boundaries shall prevent:

* semantic overlap;
* uncontrolled inheritance;
* authority ambiguity;
* conceptual duplication;
* architectural inconsistency.

Cross-domain interaction shall preserve the integrity of every participating domain.

---

### 4.7 Domain Relationships

Operational domains may participate in governed conceptual relationships.

Examples include:

* dependency;
* reference;
* interface;
* coordination;
* specialization;
* composition;
* inheritance;
* review;
* stewardship.

Relationships describe institutional meaning.

They do not independently authorize operational behavior, implementation, or authority transfer.

---

### 4.8 Parent and Child Domains

Some operational domains may conceptually organize subordinate domains.

A parent domain provides conceptual organization.

A child domain provides conceptual specialization.

Parent–child relationships organize institutional understanding.

They do not independently establish:

* operational control;
* organizational hierarchy;
* approval authority;
* implementation precedence;
* staffing responsibility.

Specialization shall preserve the conceptual integrity of both parent and child domains.

---

### 4.9 Peer Domains

Peer domains occupy equivalent conceptual levels while governing different operational subject matter.

Peer domains remain independent.

No peer domain possesses implied authority over another solely because:

* repository location;
* implementation order;
* organizational practice;
* technical integration;
* operational maturity.

Interactions among peer domains require explicitly governed relationships.

---

### 4.10 Section Determination

This portion of Section 4 establishes the conceptual foundation of operational domains and their hierarchical organization.

It authorizes:

* operational domains;
* conceptual scope;
* domain boundaries;
* domain independence;
* domain relationships;
* parent–child organization;
* peer relationships.

It does not authorize:

* organizational structures;
* runtime hierarchy;
* operational authority assignments;
* implementation architecture;
* production systems;
* operational execution.

Operational domains remain governed conceptual structures whose meaning derives from explicit institutional authority rather than implementation or organizational practice.

---

### 4.11 Domain Specialization

Domain specialization is the governed refinement of a broader operational domain into a more specific conceptual area.

A specialized domain may define:

* a narrower purpose;
* a bounded subject area;
* specialized terminology;
* specialized relationships;
* additional limitations;
* domain-specific authority dependencies.

Specialization does not create independent institutional authority merely because the specialized domain is separately represented.

The authority of a specialized domain must remain traceable to an approved governing source.

A specialized domain shall inherit applicable architectural constraints from its parent domain while preserving its own approved scope.

---

### 4.12 Domain Inheritance

Domain inheritance is the governed relationship through which a domain receives applicable concepts, constraints, principles, or relationships from another approved domain.

Inheritance may concern:

* terminology;
* architectural principles;
* required distinctions;
* relationship rules;
* historical-preservation requirements;
* technical-neutrality requirements;
* other approved conceptual conditions.

Inheritance does not automatically transfer:

* institutional authority;
* approval authority;
* responsibility;
* accountability;
* ownership;
* stewardship;
* custody;
* access;
* lifecycle status;
* operational readiness.

Inherited meaning must remain explicit and traceable.

No domain shall infer additional authority from conceptual inheritance alone.

---

### 4.13 Domain Composition

A domain may conceptually contain multiple related operational subjects or subordinate domains.

Composition provides architectural organization.

It does not establish that every composed subject:

* shares the same authority;
* shares the same lifecycle;
* shares the same readiness;
* shares the same records;
* shares the same technical implementation;
* shares the same operational status.

Each composed subject retains its own approved meaning and relationships.

Composition shall not be used to conceal distinctions that remain institutionally significant.

---

### 4.14 Domain Aggregation

Domain aggregation is the conceptual grouping of multiple domains for navigation, review, analysis, or institutional understanding.

Aggregation does not merge the aggregated domains.

An aggregate view shall not create:

* a new authority;
* a new operational domain;
* shared ownership;
* shared responsibility;
* common lifecycle status;
* common implementation requirements.

Aggregated representations must preserve the identity, scope, and authority of each participating domain.

---

### 4.15 Domain Decomposition

Domain decomposition is the governed separation of a broader conceptual domain into smaller, more specific domains or subjects.

Decomposition may support:

* clearer scope;
* improved reviewability;
* reduced ambiguity;
* specialized governance;
* more precise relationships;
* bounded implementation.

Decomposition shall not occur solely because a software system, repository structure, or organizational arrangement prefers smaller units.

A decomposition must preserve the meaning and historical continuity of the broader domain.

---

### 4.16 Domain Overlap

Domain overlap exists where two or more domains legitimately concern related aspects of the same institutional subject.

Overlap does not necessarily indicate defect.

A valid overlap may require:

* separate authority;
* distinct purposes;
* coordinated review;
* explicit interfaces;
* dependency relationships;
* bounded shared concepts.

Overlap must not be resolved by silently assigning the entire subject to one domain.

Where multiple domains govern different aspects of the same subject, each authority and boundary must remain explicit.

---

### 4.17 Domain Ambiguity

Domain ambiguity exists where the appropriate conceptual domain, authority, relationship, or boundary cannot be determined reliably.

Ambiguity may arise from:

* incomplete definitions;
* overlapping terminology;
* conflicting sources;
* unclear authority;
* historical inconsistency;
* technical labels;
* organizational practice;
* another unresolved condition.

Domain ambiguity shall be recorded rather than resolved through assumption.

The repository must not infer a governing domain solely from:

* file location;
* software ownership;
* technical access;
* organizational seniority;
* prior custom;
* the identity of the contributor.

Resolution requires appropriate institutional authority and evidence.

---

### 4.18 Domain Conflict

A domain conflict exists where two or more domains make materially incompatible claims concerning the same subject, scope, authority, relationship, or institutional meaning.

A conflict should identify:

* domains involved;
* conflicting claims;
* applicable scope;
* governing sources;
* available evidence;
* unresolved authority;
* affected relationships;
* historical context.

A domain conflict must not be resolved solely through:

* implementation precedence;
* repository hierarchy;
* technical configuration;
* organizational convenience;
* majority preference;
* latest timestamp;
* unilateral action.

The conflict shall remain preserved until resolved through competent institutional governance.

---

### 4.19 Domain Coordination

Domain coordination is the governed alignment of related conceptual or operational matters among independent domains.

Coordination may support:

* information exchange;
* dependency awareness;
* aligned review;
* relationship clarification;
* shared planning;
* conflict referral;
* historical consistency.

Coordination does not establish superior authority over participating domains.

A coordinating domain or participant does not acquire substantive authority merely by facilitating cross-domain work.

---

### 4.20 Domain Interface

A domain interface is a governed relationship through which one operational domain provides, receives, references, reviews, or depends upon another domain’s approved authority, information, records, or capability.

An interface should identify, where applicable:

* participating domains;
* interface purpose;
* information or subject exchanged;
* authority boundaries;
* responsibility boundaries;
* required evidence;
* limitations;
* historical treatment.

An interface does not automatically transfer:

* authority;
* accountability;
* ownership;
* custody;
* access;
* approval;
* lifecycle state;
* operational responsibility.

Any transfer must be separately and expressly governed.

---

### 4.21 Domain Dependency

A domain dependency exists where one domain requires an approved concept, decision, record, capability, or other institutional condition governed by another domain.

A dependency may be:

* foundational;
* informational;
* methodological;
* technical;
* administrative;
* legal or contractual;
* educational;
* another approved type.

Dependency does not establish:

* subordinate authority;
* operational control;
* readiness;
* implementation order;
* shared ownership;
* status propagation.

Each dependency shall remain explicit and reviewable.

---

### 4.22 Upstream and Downstream Relationships

The architecture may describe upstream and downstream relationships for the limited purpose of understanding conceptual dependency or information flow.

An upstream domain may provide a governing concept, requirement, decision, or source.

A downstream domain may depend upon or apply that approved source within its own scope.

Upstream does not automatically mean superior authority.

Downstream does not automatically mean subordinate institutional status.

The meaning of the relationship depends on the approved authority and subject involved.

---

### 4.23 Cross-Domain Authority

Cross-domain authority exists only where an approved governing source expressly establishes authority concerning a matter spanning more than one domain.

The presence of multiple domains does not automatically create:

* shared authority;
* joint approval;
* authority precedence;
* consolidated ownership;
* a new governing body.

Where multiple authorities are required, their respective scopes and relationships must be explicit.

This foundation does not establish permanent cross-domain decision structures.

---

### 4.24 Domain Ownership

Domain ownership is not approved as an independent institutional authority category under Foundation v1.

The term may be used descriptively only where its meaning is defined precisely.

Where used, it must not obscure distinctions among:

* substantive authority;
* process ownership;
* stewardship;
* responsibility;
* accountability;
* record custody;
* technical custody;
* repository administration.

The repository should prefer these more precise relationships rather than rely upon the ambiguous term domain ownership.

---

### 4.25 Domain Stewardship

Domain stewardship is continuing responsibility for preserving the coherence, condition, relationships, documentation, and historical continuity of an operational domain within delegated scope.

Stewardship may include:

* maintaining domain definitions;
* identifying ambiguity;
* preserving relationships;
* initiating review;
* coordinating approved changes;
* supporting historical preservation.

Stewardship does not independently confer:

* substantive authority;
* approval authority;
* authority over adjacent domains;
* technical administration;
* operational activation;
* ownership of every asset within the domain.

Substantive changes remain subject to the applicable institutional authority.

---

### 4.26 Domain Representation

Operational domains may be represented through:

* authoritative documents;
* governed records;
* registries;
* schemas;
* controlled vocabularies;
* relationship structures;
* documentation indexes;
* other approved repository mechanisms.

A technical representation of a domain is not the domain’s institutional source unless expressly established as the authoritative governing record.

Domain identifiers, folder structures, database namespaces, software modules, and interface labels remain technical representations.

They do not independently determine institutional scope or authority.

---

### 4.27 Domain Representation and Organizational Structure

Operational-domain architecture is distinct from organizational structure.

A domain may exist without:

* a dedicated department;
* a permanent role;
* a manager;
* employees;
* contractors;
* a budget;
* a reporting line.

An organizational unit may support multiple domains.

A domain may be supported by multiple organizational contexts.

Organizational structure must not be inferred from conceptual domain architecture.

---

### 4.28 Domain Representation and Technical Architecture

Operational domains are distinct from software modules, services, databases, APIs, repositories, and infrastructure components.

A technical component may support multiple operational domains.

An operational domain may be represented across multiple technical components.

Technical boundaries shall not redefine domain boundaries by implication.

Platform & Systems Development may map technical architecture to approved operational domains but may not create or alter operational-domain meaning through implementation alone.

---

### 4.29 Domain Historical Preservation

Material domain history should preserve:

* prior definitions;
* prior scope;
* prior authority relationships;
* boundary changes;
* relationship changes;
* specialization;
* decomposition;
* consolidation;
* supersession;
* retirement;
* unresolved conflicts.

Domain history shall not be silently overwritten where institutional meaning changes.

A current domain structure does not erase the validity or relevance of earlier domain structures applicable to historical records or decisions.

---

### 4.31 Domain Classification

A domain classification is an approved conceptual category used to organize operational domains according to institutional purpose or architectural function.

A classification may support:

* conceptual organization;
* repository navigation;
* relationship definition;
* review;
* dependency analysis;
* historical preservation.

A domain classification does not establish:

* institutional authority;
* organizational status;
* operational maturity;
* implementation priority;
* ownership;
* staffing;
* technical architecture.

Classification remains distinct from domain identity, scope, authority, lifecycle, and implementation.

---

### 4.32 Domain Type and Domain Instance

A domain type is a reusable conceptual category describing a recognized form of operational domain.

A domain instance is a particular approved domain established within the institutional architecture.

Approval of a domain type does not create a domain instance.

A domain instance requires its own:

* approved purpose;
* scope;
* authority basis;
* boundaries;
* relationships;
* limitations;
* historical identity.

This foundation authorizes conceptual domain architecture.

It does not create new operational-domain instances beyond those separately established by approved institutional authority.

---

### 4.33 Foundational and Specialized Domains

A foundational domain governs a broad and enduring area of institutional meaning.

A specialized domain governs a narrower subject derived from, related to, or bounded by one or more foundational domains.

Foundational status does not establish superior authority over every specialized domain.

Specialized status does not imply reduced institutional significance.

Authority depends on the approved subject and scope.

A specialized domain may possess exclusive substantive authority within its bounded subject where expressly established.

---

### 4.34 Internal and External Operational Domains

An internal operational domain concerns Huerta Group’s own institutional operations, governance, capabilities, records, or supporting structures.

An external operational domain concerns approved operations involving clients, partners, learners, vendors, service recipients, or other external contexts.

Internal and external domains remain distinct even where they use common:

* methodology;
* technology;
* processes;
* records;
* participants;
* terminology.

An internal operational capability does not become an external service by implication.

External operational architecture requires separate authority, scope, disclosure, data, legal, commercial, and relationship governance.

---

### 4.35 Substantive and Supporting Domains

A substantive domain governs the institutional meaning of a defined subject.

A supporting domain provides capability, administration, coordination, representation, technical support, custody, or another bounded form of assistance.

A supporting domain does not acquire substantive authority over the subject it supports.

A substantive domain does not automatically acquire authority over every supporting mechanism.

For example:

* Platform & Systems Development may support technical representation without governing operational doctrine.
* Repository administration may preserve records without governing their substantive meaning.
* Corporate administration may perform company-level actions without redefining operational architecture.

Each relationship must remain explicit.

---

### 4.36 Governing and Implementing Domains

A governing domain establishes approved institutional meaning, requirements, boundaries, or decisions.

An implementing domain realizes approved requirements through technical, administrative, operational, educational, communicative, or other authorized implementation.

Implementation does not transfer governing authority.

Governance does not necessarily include implementation responsibility.

The architecture must preserve traceability from implementation to governing authority.

Where implementation conflicts with the governing source, the implementation is defective.

---

### 4.37 Domain Authority Source

Every authoritative operational domain must remain traceable to an approved institutional source.

An authority source may include:

* the Constitution;
* an approved foundation;
* an approved institutional decision;
* an approved policy or standard;
* an approved delegation;
* another valid governing source.

Authority must not be inferred from:

* domain name;
* repository location;
* contributor identity;
* technical ownership;
* operational activity;
* software administration;
* historical custom;
* public description.

A domain representation records authority.

It does not create authority.

---

### 4.38 Domain Authority Scope

Domain authority applies only within the approved subject and scope.

Authority over one domain does not imply authority over:

* adjacent domains;
* supporting domains;
* subordinate technical representations;
* every asset associated with the domain;
* every process operating within the domain;
* cross-domain decisions;
* public disclosure;
* commercial activity.

Where a matter crosses domains, each applicable authority must remain identifiable.

No general authority-propagation rule is approved.

---

### 4.39 Domain Responsibility

A domain may have conceptual responsibility relationships concerning:

* stewardship;
* review;
* coordination;
* record preservation;
* implementation;
* technical support;
* dependency maintenance;
* change preparation;
* another approved responsibility.

Responsibility for a domain does not independently confer substantive authority over that domain.

Responsibility relationships require their own authority basis, scope, and evidence.

No active domain-responsibility assignments are established by this section.

---

### 4.40 Domain Lifecycle

Operational domains may require lifecycle representation concerning:

* development maturity;
* approval;
* current applicability;
* review;
* supersession;
* retirement;
* archival treatment.

These dimensions remain independent.

A domain may be:

* conceptually mature but unapproved;
* approved but not represented technically;
* technically represented but inactive operationally;
* superseded but historically significant;
* retired but preserved.

Section 9 remains authoritative for lifecycle and status architecture.

No active lifecycle status is assigned through this section.

---

### 4.41 Domain Creation

Creation of an operational domain is a governed institutional act.

A proposed domain should identify:

* purpose;
* scope;
* authority basis;
* relationship to existing domains;
* included and excluded subjects;
* dependencies;
* potential overlaps;
* limitations;
* historical rationale.

A repository folder, schema namespace, technical module, project, room, or organizational label does not independently create an operational domain.

This foundation does not create a domain-creation process or approve new domain instances.

---

### 4.42 Domain Modification

A substantive domain modification changes institutional meaning, including:

* purpose;
* scope;
* authority;
* boundaries;
* classification;
* relationships;
* dependencies;
* inheritance;
* another material condition.

Substantive modification requires governed review, authority, versioning, and historical preservation.

Technical changes that do not alter institutional meaning remain technical implementation changes.

A technical representation must not silently modify the domain’s approved meaning.

---

### 4.43 Domain Consolidation

Domain consolidation is the governed combination of two or more domains into a new or revised domain structure.

Consolidation should preserve:

* prior identities;
* prior authority;
* historical records;
* distinct subject matter;
* dependencies;
* unresolved conflicts;
* transition relationships.

Consolidation does not automatically:

* merge authority;
* transfer responsibility;
* resolve overlap;
* supersede every prior record;
* activate a successor domain.

No domain consolidation is authorized by this section.

---

### 4.44 Domain Separation

Domain separation is the governed division of an existing domain into two or more independently defined domains.

Separation may be appropriate where:

* scope has become materially distinct;
* authority differs;
* conceptual overlap creates ambiguity;
* specialization requires independent governance;
* another approved architectural condition applies.

Separation must preserve:

* origin;
* prior scope;
* authority history;
* transferred concepts;
* retained concepts;
* dependencies;
* transition;
* historical records.

No domain separation is authorized by this section.

---

### 4.45 Domain Supersession

Domain supersession records that a later approved domain definition replaces the active institutional function of an earlier domain definition within an approved scope.

Supersession should identify:

* superseded domain;
* superseding domain;
* authority;
* reason;
* effective scope;
* transition;
* relationship treatment;
* historical preservation.

Supersession does not automatically:

* erase the earlier domain;
* invalidate historical decisions;
* transfer every responsibility;
* activate technical implementation;
* retire every related asset;
* close active work.

Each consequence requires separate governance.

---

### 4.46 Domain Retirement

Domain retirement removes an approved operational domain from ordinary current use.

Retirement should identify:

* domain;
* authority;
* reason;
* effective scope;
* successor where applicable;
* dependency treatment;
* record preservation;
* continuing historical relevance.

Retirement is distinct from:

* supersession;
* archival;
* deletion;
* organizational dissolution;
* technical shutdown.

No domain retirement is authorized by this section.

---

### 4.47 Domain Archival Treatment

An archived domain record preserves historical institutional meaning after the domain is no longer current.

Archival treatment may preserve:

* prior scope;
* prior authority;
* relationships;
* decisions;
* changes;
* conflicts;
* supersession;
* retirement.

Archival status does not establish:

* deletion authority;
* unrestricted access;
* retention period;
* destruction eligibility;
* loss of institutional significance.

Historical domain records remain relevant to interpretation of earlier operational assets and decisions.

---

### 4.48 Cross-Domain Non-Propagation

A condition established in one domain does not automatically establish the same condition in another domain.

For example:

* approval in one domain does not approve another domain;
* maturity in one domain does not establish maturity elsewhere;
* technical readiness does not establish operational readiness;
* authority over a source domain does not create authority over an adaptation;
* retirement of one domain does not retire supporting domains;
* a domain conflict does not invalidate every related record;
* shared terminology does not create shared institutional meaning.

Any propagation requires an expressly approved governing rule.

---

### 4.49 Cross-System Domain Neutrality

A domain represented in one repository, platform, database, environment, or software system does not automatically possess the same representation, status, scope, or authority in another system.

Cross-system representation requires:

* stable identifiers;
* semantic mapping;
* source-of-authority rules;
* version alignment;
* provenance;
* scope;
* conflict handling;
* historical preservation.

Technical synchronization does not create new institutional authority.

A copied domain record remains subordinate to its authoritative source.

---

### 4.50 Domain Identifier Neutrality

A domain identifier is a technical or repository mechanism used to reference a governed domain.

An identifier does not independently establish:

* authority;
* hierarchy;
* scope;
* organizational ownership;
* lifecycle status;
* technical ownership;
* operational activity.

Identifiers should remain stable where practical.

A change in identifier must preserve historical traceability and must not be used to conceal a substantive domain change.

---

### 4.51 Domain Naming Neutrality

A domain name supports institutional understanding and reference.

A name does not independently determine:

* authority;
* scope;
* organizational status;
* maturity;
* implementation;
* ownership.

Names should reflect approved institutional meaning.

A software label, folder name, project name, room name, vendor term, or public-facing phrase does not automatically become the approved institutional domain name.

### 4.53 Prohibited Domain Inferences

The repository and future technical systems must not infer that:

* a domain is a department;
* a parent domain possesses authority over every child domain;
* a child domain inherits all parent authority;
* peer domains possess equivalent authority;
* dependency creates subordination;
* coordination creates governance;
* overlap creates conflict automatically;
* aggregation creates a new domain;
* composition merges authority;
* specialization creates independent authority;
* stewardship creates substantive authority;
* repository location establishes domain membership;
* a technical namespace establishes institutional scope;
* a software owner is a domain authority holder;
* a domain identifier creates institutional legitimacy;
* public language creates an internal operational domain;
* repeated practice creates a domain;
* shared records merge domains;
* shared personnel merge domains;
* shared technology merges domains;
* a domain status propagates to another domain;
* supersession erases historical authority;
* retirement authorizes deletion.

Each conclusion requires its own approved authority, scope, context, and evidence.

---

### 4.54 Technical Metadata Neutrality

Schemas, namespaces, folders, identifiers, registries, project structures, software modules, organizational charts, database models, and technical labels may represent approved domain architecture.

They do not replace, redefine, or become the authoritative institutional meaning of:

* domain;
* scope;
* hierarchy;
* authority;
* stewardship;
* dependency;
* interface;
* lifecycle;
* supersession;
* retirement.

Where technical metadata conflicts with the approved domain definition, the technical representation is defective and must be corrected.

---

### 4.55 Conceptual and Active Domain Records

The architecture must distinguish among:

Domain definition

A governed description of a domain’s meaning, purpose, scope, authority, boundaries, and relationships.

Domain type

A reusable conceptual category within domain architecture.

Conceptual domain relationship

A repository representation of a possible or approved relationship among domains.

Active operational domain

A domain under which actual institutional operations are authorized and performed.

Historical domain record

A prior domain definition, relationship, or status preserved for traceability.

This foundation authorizes domain definitions, types, conceptual relationships, and historical architecture.

It does not authorize active operational-domain execution.
